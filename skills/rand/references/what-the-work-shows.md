# What the work actually shows

This file records what was seen by looking at the images in the archive at paulrand.design (`https://assets.paulrand.design/Works/...`), not what is said about him. Looked at for this record: all 132 logos on the Logos page, 120 of the 200 book-cover images, all 21 posters, 36 of the 71 paintings, the photography page, and the first 24 pages of the American Express logo presentation book. Re-look before designing (see "How to study the archive").

## The headline finding
There is no single Rand style. A book cover, a cigar-band mark, a computer logo and a poster look like four different designers' work. What repeats is a **method of getting an idea out of a subject with the fewest, simplest, most confident marks**, plus a recognisable **vocabulary** (below). Imitating a "look" produces clip art. Using the method and the vocabulary produces something that could sit in the archive.

## What it is not
- It is not glossy, gradient, shadowed or 3D. Across 132 logos and 140 covers and posters the surfaces are flat, solid colour. Depth appears only as **overlap of flat shapes** (cut paper laid over cut paper), scale, and the real shadow of a photographed paper collage.
- It is not one typeface. It is Didone (Bodoni-like), slab, condensed grotesque, hand script, typewriter and rough hand lettering, often in the same piece.
- It is not tidy for its own sake. Precise geometry sits beside loose hand lines in the same mark.

## The vocabulary (each seen several times)

**1. A solid shape as the container.** The mark lives inside or is cut by one big simple shape: black circle (ABC, Helbros, Glass Packer, AIGA 1982 versions, PowerOpen/PowerPC symbols, Yale, PDR), ellipse (Century Lighting, Cabbages & Kings), plain square (Borzoi yellow, Esquire pink, Elliot Noyes brown), diamond (Gibson Diamond, Irwin Financial), shield (UPS), capsule or rounded oval (Ford, IBM Product Center), tilted square (The Limited, NeXT cube, OS/2). The shape carries the colour and the weight; the letters are small and exact inside it.

**2. Colour blocking letters.** Letters are made by cutting them out of or laying them against flat blocks of colour: Harcourt Brace (an H cut from a blue block, a B from a red block, side by side), Elliot Noyes (an E and N from one brown block and a few lines), Servador (letters sliced across in three colours), Mossberg (letters as stripes in primary colours), Tipton Lakes (the T is a green block with white ducks cut out of it). The block is the letter.

**3. The rebus: a letter becomes an object.** Helbros (the o is a watch spring spiral in a black disc), AIGA 1982 (the A-I-G-A with an eye as the I), Gentry (the g is stacked coloured bands), XGA (letters on a map-pin shape over a dot, an exclamation mark), Columbus Indiana (a circle of rotating Cs), Consolidated Cigar (rings with Cs inside), Creative Media Center (C, M, C as coloured capsules), Dunhill (tall black capitals each wearing a small hat, so the letters are men), Esquire (a face drawn with two circles and three lines), Zebra Press and Thomas Creighton (one silhouette: a swirl, a swan), Atlas (a triangle that is an A with the name inside it), Irwin Financial (a diamond made of numerals). The wit is always **one substitution**, and the viewer completes it.

**4. Type with one decision per letter.** Kaiser (each letter in its own colour and shape), Ohrbach's (a black strip with the letters knocked out and spaced), Morningstar (the O replaced by a thin round arc), PowerOpen (the O drawn as a plain circle outline), IITRI (outlined thin letters then solid R and I), Cresta Blanca (condensed, inked, rough), Dubonnet (heavy red condensed capitals with a small script line under). One typographic idea, applied to the whole word.

**5. Hand lettering and hand-drawn line against geometry.** Colorforms (a loose script beside a tiny coloured figure), Country Club ice cream (script with yellow dots), Maidenform, Pacific Coast Borax and Portfolio (brush and pen script), Wallace Puppets (a hand drawn from one line), Jacqueline Cochran, James E. Pepper, Coronet (a brandy snifter drawn as a character in thin line). The hand supplies warmth; the geometry supplies order.

**6. Characters from a few shapes.** Triangles, circles and rectangles with thin-line arms and legs: Shuredge (chef, owl, reindeer, Santa, turkey, each a coloured triangle with a face), Lee Hats' rabbit, Dubouchett's harlequin with a guitar, El Producto's cigar man, Century Lighting's figure with a lamp for a body, Kaiser-Frazer's service-station man. A child could draw them, and that is the point.

**7. Repetition with one exception.** Earth Day 1995 (a grid of green hearts, one red), IBM (horizontal bars), American Express' presentation book (a field of blue squares), PowerPC (a ring repeating a letter). The exception makes the rhythm visible.

**8. Extreme scale contrast.** DADA (giant overlapping black capitals), léger (a huge lowercase condensed word with a small bolt), YOGA (giant letters with a pink block), MANN (a huge black condensed name on a coloured band). Giant against tiny, and letters allowed to overlap.

**9. Cut paper and brushed ink.** The covers and posters are collage: shapes cut and torn from coloured paper, rotated, overlapped, with a black brush blot, a scribbled outline or a hand-written title laid on top (The Revelations of Dr. Modesto: a black ink-blot shape with two eyes; Painting and Reality: black and blue brush shapes; Death Mask: a skull and olive branch in three colours). Edges are cut, not rendered.

**10. A small saturated palette.** Black and white anchors, and from a short list: hot pink, orange-red, red, yellow, cobalt, kelly green, cream, tan. Two to four colours per piece, flat, each doing one job.

**11. A silhouette that survives.** Ice-cream cone (a pink half-disc and a tan triangle), a heart in a tan circle, a skull, a swan, a vase, an owl. Ideas are one or two shapes.

## The process, as the archive shows it
- **Sketch many, ugly, fast.** Four pages of loose pen skull sketches precede the finished Death Mask poster (blue, black, white with an olive branch). The final is the simplest of them.
- **The paintings use the same vocabulary** (blobs with eyes, a cat as a circle and two ears, a fish as an almond). The vocabulary is a way of seeing, not a client style.
- **The presentation book sells with restraint** (American Express): a black cover with a few white line polygons; pages that are mostly empty grey with a short paragraph; the mark introduced small, then large, then shown in a grid of repeats, on a T-shirt cut out of red paper, on a card. One idea per spread, and the application does the persuading.

## What this means for work made "in his spirit"
1. Start with the subject's own object and find the one substitution (3), the container (1), or the block (2) that makes the name appear.
2. Build with flat colour, overlap and scale. If depth is asked for, build it as layers of flat paper with a hard offset shadow, not gradients or gloss.
3. Let a hand element and a geometric element share the mark (5, 6, 9).
4. Make the viewer complete the idea (3, 7).
5. Use scale and silhouette, not detail (8, 11).
6. Keep a limited, saturated palette (10).
7. Present the mark in a grid of repeats and in use, with room around it.

## How to study the archive before designing
1. List the page: `https://www.paulrand.design/work/Logos.html` (also Book-Covers, Posters, Logo-Presentation-Books, Painting, Photography, Other-Work). Image URLs are in `src="https://assets.paulrand.design/Works/..."`; logos are SVG, the rest webp.
2. Download the images for the category into a scratch folder, one request each, with a normal browser user agent.
3. Build contact sheets (an HTML grid of `<img>` tags, 24 to 40 per sheet, labelled), render them with a headless browser, and look at the pictures.
4. Write down what you see, in the terms above, before drawing anything. Do not describe a mark you have not looked at.
5. Look at 40 or more pieces from at least three categories each time the skill is used for a new project.
