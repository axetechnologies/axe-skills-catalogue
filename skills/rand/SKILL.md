# Rand

**Read `references/what-the-work-shows.md` first.** It records what the archive's images actually contain (flat cut-paper shapes, containers, colour-blocked letters, rebus, characters, scale, one saturated palette). Before using this skill on a new piece, look at 40 or more of his works from at least three categories (method in that file), and never describe a mark you have not looked at.

A working method for making visual work that is simple, specific and a little witty. It is modelled on Paul Rand (1914-1996: IBM, ABC, UPS, Westinghouse, NeXT, Cummins, Yale University Press, Morningstar, Colorforms, plus book jackets, posters, ads and a body of writing). It is a method and a taste, not a costume. Do not imitate his marks; apply his reasoning to the client in front of you.

Use it for any front-end work. The principles are about decisions, so they apply in HTML/CSS, React, native, slides, print and motion alike.

## The stance (non-negotiable)

1. **Form and content are one thing.** Design is the integration of the beautiful and the useful. A decoration that carries no meaning is removed, and a function with no care in its form is unfinished.
2. **One idea per piece.** Find the single true thing about the subject and say it once. If a page, mark or screen needs a second idea to hold up, the first one is not found yet.
3. **Simplicity is a result, not a goal.** It comes from a good idea expressed modestly. Subtract until removing one more thing would break the meaning, then stop.
4. **Wit is a tool.** A visual pun, a rebus, a surprising shape that is also the obvious one. Play must serve recognition, never distract from it.
5. **Do not try to be original, try to be good.** Originality that is not good is noise. Good work that follows a sound principle will be distinct anyway because the subject is.
6. **A mark identifies, it does not sell.** A logo is a flag, a signature, a street sign. Its meaning is built by the company's behaviour. Do not ask it to explain the business.
7. **The client has a problem, not a taste.** Understand the audience and the job before drawing anything. Ignore committee preference; defend the idea with reasons.

## The process

Run these in order. Write the output of each step down; the writing is the design.

1. **Brief in four lines.** Who is it for? What must it do in three seconds? What is the one true thing? What must never happen? If these cannot be answered, ask.
2. **Analyse.** List what the subject is made of: tools, materials, gestures, units, vocabulary, the objects people already associate with it. This is the raw material for puns and shapes.
3. **Interpret.** Choose the one idea. Phrase it as a sentence: "A painter's roller is also the stroke of an M." If it needs "and", it is two ideas.
4. **Formulate.** Build it from primitives (circle, square, triangle, line, bar), a restrained palette and one or two typefaces. Draw many versions fast. Ten bad ones before one good one.
5. **Simplify.** Remove detail, weight, colours and effects until the idea is exposed. Square the geometry. Fix optical errors by eye, not by number.
6. **Test** (see below). A piece that fails a test goes back to step 3 or 4, not to decoration.
7. **Present with the story.** Rand's logo presentation books told the idea in a few pages: the problem, the idea, the mark in use. Show the work in context, with one recommendation and the reason.

## The iteration loop

Render, look, critique, revise. Never judge a design from its code. Render it at desktop and at 390px, look at the image, write down the three worst problems, fix only those, and repeat at least twice before showing it. Score the result with the rubric in `references/craft-and-iteration.md` (idea, name, geometry, type, depth, memorability, versatility, system, restraint, mobile). Under 60 is not shown.

When the user gives a number or a feeling ("30 percent there", "zero depth", "not premium"), read it as a score on a dimension: say what was weak in the last round in two or three plain lines, change the approach (usually fewer concepts developed further, initials inside the mark, a designed wordmark, a system), and do not just add decoration.

## The tests

Run every mark and every key screen through these before showing it.

- **Squint / blur test.** Blur the image or step back. The silhouette must still read and still be the right thing.
- **One-colour test.** Render in black on white, then white on black. Meaning must not depend on colour.
- **Small test.** 16px favicon, 32px app icon, a business-card corner. If it clogs, simplify.
- **Distance test.** On a screen, is the point of the page clear in three seconds from across the room?
- **Fax test.** Imagine a poor reproduction: flat, low-res, no gradients. Does it survive?
- **Reversal and rotation.** Does it hold upside-down, mirrored in your head, on a coloured ground?
- **Name test.** Cover the name. Does the mark still point at the right trade? Cover the mark. Does the name carry?
- **Ten-year test.** Would you still be willing to see it in ten years? If it follows a trend, it will date.

## Visual vocabulary

The measured vocabulary is in `references/what-the-work-shows.md` (container shapes, colour-blocked letters, rebus, type with one decision per letter, hand lettering against geometry, characters from shapes, repetition with one exception, extreme scale, cut paper and brushed ink). The notes below are the working rules drawn from it.

- **Geometry first.** Circle, square, triangle, bar, arc. Build letters and objects from them. Use true geometry for structure and a human touch (a drip, a loose stroke, an italic) in one place only.
- **Flat, solid colour.** Across 130 logos and 140 covers and posters nothing is glossy, gradient or 3D. Depth is overlap of flat paper shapes, scale and the real shadow of cut paper. If depth is asked for, build it as layers of flat paper with a hard offset shadow (see Depth). Never use effects to rescue a weak idea.
- **Palette.** One or two strong colours plus black and white, used plainly. Primary red, blue and yellow are a valid answer when the subject is playful or technical; a single signature colour is often better. Name every colour and give it a job.
- **Contrast.** Big and small, black and white, round and square, rough and smooth, solid and line. Design is chosen contrasts. Use few, use them hard.
- **Asymmetry.** Bilateral symmetry is predictable and asks nothing of the viewer. Balanced asymmetry (a heavy element offset by a light one, space against mass) holds attention.
- **Repetition and rhythm.** Repeat an element to build texture, speed and order: bars, dots, stripes, numerals, modules. Break the pattern once for emphasis.
- **Space.** White space is a material, not what is left over. Give the idea room. Group by distance before you group by boxes.
- **Scale.** Few sizes, large steps. Three type sizes and a clear ratio beat nine near-sizes.
- **Humour and surprise.** Substitute one part of a familiar object with another (a rebus). Keep the substitution obvious at first glance.

## Depth, material and light

His depth is the depth of cut paper: shapes laid over shapes. When a brief asks for dimension, build it that way first.

- **Overlap and layer** flat shapes. The order of the layers is the depth.
- **A hard offset shadow** (no blur, 2-4 units down and right, 15-20 percent black) makes a shape read as paper lying on paper. One direction for the whole piece.
- **Scale and silhouette** do more than shading: a huge shape behind a small one, a shape cropped by the edge.
- **Texture from the hand**: a torn edge, a brushed edge, a halftone dot field, a scribbled outline.
- **Only if the client wants rendered objects** (gloss, metal, glass, extrusion), build them as a separate variant with one light source, three values per form and a soft ground shadow, and keep the flat paper version as the primary. Say plainly that the rendered version is a departure from his work.
- **In an interface**, depth means hierarchy and state: raised things are interactive or important, pressed things sit lower, overlays cast the biggest shadow. Keep the shadows soft and consistent, and disable motion under reduced-motion.

## Typography

- **One display face and one text face, or a single family.** A third needs a reason.
- **Choose by temperament.** Humanist serif (Garamond style) for warmth and authority. Didone (Bodoni style) for contrast and elegance. Slab or egyptian for strength and engineering. Neo-grotesque or geometric sans for clarity and modernity.
- **Web mapping (free families):** Garamond - EB Garamond. Bodoni - Bodoni Moda. Slab - Alfa Slab One, Zilla Slab, Rokkitt. Heavy grotesque - Archivo Black. Neutral sans - Archivo, Work Sans. Geometric - Jost. Always declare a fallback stack.
- **Set it with care.** Tight tracking on large display type, open tracking on small caps and labels. Sentence case for reading, capitals only for short labels. Measure of 55-70 characters. Real italics, no fake ones. Optical alignment on large type.
- **Type is the image** when there is no better picture. A bold italic with one coloured full stop can be a complete identity.

## Applying it to front-end work

Translate the principles into decisions a screen can carry. Details and snippets are in `references/frontend-recipes.md`.

- **Tokens first.** Define the palette (4-6 named colours), type (2 roles), spacing scale, radius and motion durations as variables before writing a component. Every visual decision comes from a token.
- **Hierarchy by contrast.** Make the primary thing larger or heavier by a clear step, not a hair. One primary action per view.
- **Layout.** Use a simple grid with deliberate asymmetry: a wide column and a narrow one, a large image against a small block of text. Generous margins. Align to a few shared edges.
- **Components say what they do.** Buttons are verbs ("Book the visit"), errors say what to do next, empty states say what will appear. Copy is design material.
- **Illustration and icons from primitives.** Draw in SVG with a shared stroke width and corner rule. Use `currentColor` and CSS variables so marks recolour for dark mode and one-colour use.
- **Motion with purpose.** Animate to explain order, state or cause: a reveal in reading order, a count-up to a result, a stroke that paints on. Short, eased, once. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **Themes.** Design light and dark together from the same tokens. Re-check contrast; do not invert blindly.
- **Accessibility is part of the form.** Contrast, focus states, tap targets, semantics, text that scales. A beautiful thing nobody can use has failed.
- **Performance is part of the form.** Prefer SVG and CSS shapes over images, subset fonts, no decorative weight.

## Mobile is part of the design

Every visual is checked at 360px and 390px: no horizontal scroll, tap targets 44px or more, readable type, depth lighter on phones (smaller blur, fewer layers), parallax off on touch and under reduced motion, sticky elements that never cover content or anchors. Fixed widths become `min()` or `max-width: 100%`; grid and flex children that hold text or images get `min-width: 0`.

## Logo work

1. Brief and analyse (above). List ten objects, gestures or tools from the trade.
2. Sketch at least ten ideas in three families: **letterform** (the initials made into something), **symbol** (an abstract or pictorial shape) and **rebus/character** (a picture that says the name or a figure that stands for it).
3. Cut to three. Draw them cleanly on a grid with a consistent stroke and radius.
4. Run every test. Kill the ones that need colour, detail or explanation.
5. Build the system: mark, wordmark, lockups (stacked, horizontal), one-colour, reversed, minimum size, clear space, favicon, and a short usage page.
6. Present as a story: the problem, the idea in one sentence, the mark in use (sign, van, invoice, web header, app icon), the recommendation. Use `assets/logo-sheet-template.html` for a quick specimen sheet of many ideas, and `assets/identity-sheet-template.html` for a developed identity (spec, variants, mockups) of a few finalists. For the chosen mark, build the full booklet with `references/identity-booklet.md` and `assets/identity-booklet-template.mjs`.
7. Develop fewer concepts further rather than many at the same depth. The initials or name belong inside the mark. A designed wordmark and a flat version are required.

## Critique checklist

Score each 0-2. Anything with a 0 is fixed before shipping.

- One idea, statable in one sentence
- Form carries meaning; nothing is merely decorative
- Passes squint, one-colour and small tests
- Strong contrast in at most two or three dimensions
- Asymmetry or deliberate rhythm, not default centring
- Palette limited and every colour has a job
- Type limited, set with care, one clear hierarchy
- Copy is plain, specific and says what happens
- Works in light and dark, at phone width, with keyboard
- Would still be acceptable in ten years

## Anti-patterns

- Effects standing in for an idea: gradients, glows, shadows, glassy blurs, 3D bevels.
- Symmetry and centring by default; everything the same size.
- Too many colours, typefaces, weights or icon styles.
- Stock illustration and stock phrases ("empower", "seamless", "unleash").
- A mark that explains the whole business, or needs its caption to be understood.
- Copying a famous mark's skeleton (stripes, shield and bow, interlocking letters). Take the reasoning, draw a new answer.
- Design by committee, or by one person's favourite colour.

## Output contract

When this skill is used:

1. State the one idea in a sentence before showing work.
2. Show options in families, not variations of one thing, and say which you recommend and why.
3. Show each visual at its working size and at small size, and in one colour.
4. Keep claims about Rand to what is documented in `references/principles.md` or observed in the archive (`references/what-the-work-shows.md`). Mark paraphrases as paraphrases. Do not invent quotes. Do not describe any piece you have not looked at.
5. Deliver real, usable output (SVG, HTML/CSS, tokens), not descriptions of it.
